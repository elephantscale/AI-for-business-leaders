## Course Description

Artificial intelligence has moved from a research curiosity to a line item on every
executive agenda. The hard part is no longer access to the technology — it is knowing
**where it creates value, what it costs, what it risks, and how to lead an organization
through the change.**

This course gives business leaders the vocabulary, judgment, and tools to integrate AI
into their organizations while ensuring ethical and responsible use. Leaders leave able
to hold a credible conversation with their technical teams, tell a real AI opportunity
from a demo, sponsor an AI project that actually ships, and put guardrails around it.

The course is **hands-on but code-free**. Every exercise uses no-code AI tools that a
non-programmer can run in a browser. Leaders do not learn to build AI — they learn to
*use* it personally, and to *lead* it organizationally.

The course is also **interactive by design**. Roughly half the time is discussion,
workshops, and structured exercises where participants apply each idea to their own
organization. Participants leave with artifacts, not just notes: a prioritized use-case
portfolio, a maturity assessment, a risk register, and a one-page AI initiative pitch.

---

## Why This Course Matters

Most organizations do not fail at AI because the technology doesn't work. They fail
because:

- leaders cannot tell a genuine use case from an impressive demo
- pilots multiply and nothing reaches production ("pilot purgatory")
- the data foundation was never built, so every project restarts from zero
- the workforce quietly refuses to adopt what nobody explained to them
- nobody defined what "responsible use" means until after an incident
- the business case was written in hype and could not survive a budget review

Each of those is a **leadership** failure, not an engineering one. This course targets
exactly those failure modes.

# Day 1 — Understanding AI and Finding the Value

## Module 1 — What AI Actually Is (and Isn't)
*Exercise 1 — The Jargon Decoder*

- Course orientation; how this course works and what you'll walk out with
- A short, honest history: from rules, to machine learning, to foundation models
- What a large language model actually does — prediction, not knowledge
- The terminology leaders must own: model, prompt, context window, token, training vs.
  inference, fine-tuning, RAG, agent, multimodal, open vs. closed models
- Why AI got cheap and fast — and what that changes about strategy
- The current landscape: who the major providers are, and how to think about choosing
- What is *not* AI: automation, dashboards, and rules engines wearing a costume

> **Exercise 1 — The Jargon Decoder.** Teams are handed real sentences from vendor
> pitches, press releases, and internal memos. Which claims are meaningful, which are
> vague, and which are wrong? Each team rewrites one claim into something a technical
> team could actually be held to.

## Module 2 — Working With AI: Prompting as a Leadership Skill
*Exercise 2 — The Prompting Workshop*

- The single highest-leverage skill: the same model, better instructions, better result
- Principle 1 — write clear and specific instructions: delimiters, structured output,
  asking for a specific format, giving the model an example
- Principle 2 — give the model time to think: step-by-step reasoning, self-checking
- Iterative refinement: your first prompt is a draft, not a verdict
- The four workhorse patterns for business work:
  **summarize** (long report → executive brief), **infer** (customer feedback → themes
  and sentiment), **transform** (rewrite for audience, translate, reformat),
  **expand** (bullet points → a full draft)
- Context engineering without the jargon: what to hand the model and in what order
- Where your data goes: what to never paste, and why

> **Exercise 2 — The Prompting Workshop.** A guided sequence adapted from our developer
> course, rebuilt for a chat window and no code. Participants take a long document
> through summarize → infer → transform → expand, then run a head-to-head: the naive
> prompt versus the engineered prompt on the same task. The gap is the lesson.

## Module 3 — Capabilities, Limits, and Trust
*Exercise 3 — Spot the Failure*

- What today's AI is genuinely excellent at
- What it is unreliable at, and what it simply cannot do
- Hallucination: what it is, why it happens, and why it will never fully go away
- Confidence is not accuracy — the most dangerous property of these systems
- How teams reduce the risk: retrieval and grounding (RAG), citations, human review,
  narrow scope, structured output, evaluation
- Deciding the stakes: where a wrong answer is an inconvenience, and where it is
  unacceptable — the reversibility test
- Human-in-the-loop as a design choice, not a disclaimer

> **Exercise 3 — Spot the Failure.** Participants receive AI-generated outputs that
> look excellent and are subtly wrong. Find the errors, then classify: would our current
> process have caught this? Teams then set a trust threshold for three tasks in their
> own area.

## Module 4 — Finding and Prioritizing Business Value
*Exercise 4 — The Use-Case Portfolio*

- Where AI value actually comes from: cost, speed, quality, capacity, and new capability
- The patterns that repeat across every industry:
  document understanding, search over internal knowledge, drafting and summarization,
  classification and routing, extraction, quality review, decision support, and
  conversational assistance
- Use cases in regulated and safety-critical environments: what changes, what doesn't
- Sourcing candidates: the friction audit, the "where do we retype things" test
- Scoring: business value × feasibility × data readiness × risk
- The portfolio view: quick wins, strategic bets, and things to refuse
- Anti-patterns: the solution looking for a problem, the demo that can't scale, the
  project with no owner

> **Exercise 4 — The Use-Case Portfolio.** Teams generate candidate use cases from
> their own organization, score them on the value/feasibility grid, and defend one
> "quick win" and one "strategic bet" to the room. The room votes. Output: a real
> prioritized portfolio, carried into Day 2.

**Day 1 wrap-up:** what surprised you, what you'll stop believing, and the one thing
you'll try tomorrow.

# Day 2 — Leading the Organization Through AI

## Module 5 — AI Strategy and the Stages of AI Success
*Exercise 5 — Honest Maturity Assessment*

- Strategy first: AI serves the business objective, not the reverse
- The stages organizations move through — experimenting, piloting, operationalizing,
  scaling, transforming — and the failure mode at each stage
- Why organizations get stuck in pilot purgatory, and how to get out
- The foundations that determine your ceiling: data availability, data quality, access,
  and infrastructure
- Centralized, federated, or embedded — how to organize the AI capability
- Setting measurable objectives; what to track and what vanity metrics to ignore
- Building the roadmap: sequencing capability, not just projects

> **Exercise 5 — Honest Maturity Assessment.** Each participant scores their
> organization against the stage model and the foundational readiness checklist, then
> compares with the group. The gap between self-assessment and evidence is the
> discussion.

## Module 6 — Building an AI-Ready Culture
*Exercise 6 — The Resistance Role Play*

- Why adoption, not technology, is the binding constraint
- The four reactions to AI: enthusiasm, indifference, fear, and quiet sabotage
- Talking honestly about jobs — what changes, what doesn't, and what leaders owe people
- Skills and roles: what to hire, what to train, what to partner for
- Making experimentation safe: sanctioned tools, sandboxes, and the alternative to
  shadow AI
- Incentives and recognition: rewarding the person who automates their own task
- Communication: what leaders must say, repeatedly, in their own words

> **Exercise 6 — The Resistance Role Play.** Participants take roles — the skeptical
> senior engineer, the anxious analyst, the enthusiastic overreacher, the compliance
> officer — and one leader must carry an AI initiative through the conversation. Rotate,
> then debrief what actually worked.

## Module 7 — Responsible AI, Risk, and Governance
*Exercise 7 — The Incident Tabletop*

- What "responsible AI" means in practice: accountability, transparency, fairness,
  privacy, security, and human oversight
- The real risks, ranked: incorrect output, data leakage, over-reliance, bias,
  intellectual property, vendor lock-in, and reputational exposure
- Data handling: what may be entered into which tool, retention, and residency —
  and the difference that actually matters, consumer accounts versus an enterprise
  tenant your organization controls
- Security for leaders, demonstrated rather than described: an ordinary-looking document
  hijacks a working assistant, live, in front of the room
- Why it worked, and why it cannot be patched — the model cannot distinguish your
  instruction from text you gave it to read
- Which of your existing security instincts transfer (untrusted input, least privilege,
  approval gates, logging) and which do not (the attack is written in plain English,
  and the failure is silent and plausible)
- Poisoned content, excessive agency, and why "the AI did it" is not a defense
- The governing principle for agents: a system that only produces text for a human to
  act on is a fundamentally smaller problem than one that can act by itself
- Governance that works: an AI use policy people can actually follow, an approval path
  proportional to risk, an inventory of AI in use, logging, and audit
- The regulatory landscape at a leader's altitude, and how to stay adaptable
- Assigning accountability: who owns the output of a machine?

> **Exercise 7 — The Incident Tabletop.** A realistic scenario unfolds in stages — an
> AI-assisted document goes out with a fabricated reference in it. Teams work the
> response: detect, contain, communicate, remediate, and prevent. Output: three
> governance controls each team would put in place on Monday.

## Module 8 — Planning, Investing, and No-Code Enablement
*Exercise 8 — The Business Case*

- From use case to project: scope, owner, success criteria, and a kill switch
- Buy, configure, or build — and the underrated fourth option: no-code, done by the
  business itself
- What no-code AI can genuinely do today: a custom assistant grounded on your own
  documents, workflow automation, drafting and review, analysis and reporting —
  built live in front of the room, in minutes, by someone who cannot program
- The limits of no-code, and the honest signals that you now need engineering
- What AI actually costs: licensing, usage, integration, data work, change management,
  and ongoing evaluation — the iceberg under the subscription fee
- Building the business case: value hypothesis, cost, risk, time to value, and the
  measurement plan
- Vendor evaluation: the questions that separate substance from a demo
- Pilot design: small, time-boxed, measurable, with a defined decision at the end

> **Exercise 8 — The Business Case.** Teams take their Day 1 quick win and build the
> one-page case: problem, approach, value, cost, risk, controls, and the 90-day pilot.

## Closing — Pitch and Challenge
*Capstone*

Each team pitches its AI initiative in five minutes to the room. The rest of the room
acts as the investment committee, using a challenge checklist drawn from all eight
modules: Is this a real use case? What happens when it's wrong? Who owns it? What does
it cost? How will you know it worked? What would make you stop?

Participants leave with the pitch, the portfolio, the maturity assessment, and a
30/60/90-day personal action plan.

Running engagement devices used throughout:

- **Live demonstrations** — the instructor runs the tool in front of the room, including
  the failures: iterating a prompt from useless to usable, a grounded assistant built
  from nothing in ten minutes, and that same assistant hijacked by a hidden instruction
  in an ordinary document
- **Myth or reality** — quick-fire claims the room votes on, then unpacks
- **Ask me anything** — a standing, open question board addressed at every break
- **Parking lot** — organization-specific questions captured and answered before close

# Tooling

All exercises are **no-code and browser-based**. The material is written
**tool-agnostically** — the slides say "your chat assistant," not a brand — so it
survives a change of vendor. The worked examples and screenshots use the
**OpenAI / ChatGPT and Microsoft Azure** stack, which is what most enterprises have
approved and deployed.

- **Conversational assistant** — ChatGPT (or the organization's equivalent) for the
  prompting, summarizing, inference, transformation, and drafting exercises
- **Document-grounded assistant** — upload a document set and ask questions of it with
  citations; demonstrates retrieval and grounding without code. Shown with ChatGPT file
  upload and a Custom GPT built live in front of the room
- **Everyday copilots** — Microsoft 365 Copilot inside Word, Outlook, Excel, and Teams:
  AI where people already work
- **Enterprise deployment, at a leader's altitude** — Azure OpenAI and the Azure AI
  services: what it means to run these models inside your own tenant, and why that
  changes the data-handling conversation
- **No-code workflow automation** — Power Automate / Copilot Studio: putting an AI step
  into an actual business process without engineering

Every exercise has a paper-based fallback so the course runs even where network access,
approved tooling, or accounts are constrained.

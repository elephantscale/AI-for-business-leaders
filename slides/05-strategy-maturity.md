# AI Strategy and the Stages of AI Success

Elephant Scale

---

## Welcome to Day 2

Yesterday: what it is, how to use it, where it breaks, where the value is.

Today: **how to actually get an organization to do it.**

That is a different problem, and a harder one. The technology is the easy part.

Notes:

Open by clearing the parking lot from Day 1 before starting. It shows the board is real.

---

## The Uncomfortable Statistic

Most organizations that start AI projects have very little in production to show for it.

The technology worked. The pilots succeeded. Nothing shipped.

> The failure is almost never technical. It is **strategic, organizational, and
> operational** — which is to say, it is yours.

Notes:

Avoid quoting a specific survey percentage — the numbers vary wildly and get outdated.
The pattern is the point and nobody in the room will dispute it.

---

## Strategy First: The Direction of the Sentence

**Wrong:** *"We need an AI strategy."*

**Right:** *"We have a strategy. Where does AI make it achievable?"*

AI is not a destination. It is a means, and it is one of several. Sometimes the right
answer is hiring someone, fixing a process, or buying different software.

> If your AI strategy would still make sense with the words "AI" removed and something
> else substituted, it's a business strategy. Good. If it collapses, it was a technology
> shopping list.

Notes:

This test is a keeper. Run it live on any strategy statement someone in the room offers.

---

## Three Questions Any AI Strategy Must Answer

1. **What business objective does this serve?**
   Named, measurable, already on the plan.

2. **What must be true for it to work?**
   Data, skills, access, governance, sponsorship, budget.

3. **How will we know, and when will we decide?**
   A date and a criterion, before you start.

If any of the three is missing, you have an aspiration, not a strategy.

# The Stages of AI Success

---

## Five Stages, and the Trap in Each

| Stage | What's happening | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Experimenting** | Individuals trying tools | Never becomes organizational |
| **2. Piloting** | Sanctioned projects, small scope | Pilot purgatory |
| **3. Operationalizing** | First things running for real | Ownership vacuum |
| **4. Scaling** | Repeatable, multiple areas | Fragmentation, shadow sprawl |
| **5. Transforming** | Processes redesigned around it | Complacency |

**You cannot skip stages.** You can move through them faster, and organizations that
try to jump from 1 to 4 land back at 1 with less credibility.

Notes:

Ask the room to place their organization before you explain the stages. Then explain.
Then ask again. The answer usually moves down.

---

## Stage 1 — Experimenting

**What it looks like:** individuals using AI tools on their own initiative. Enthusiasm
in pockets. Usually invisible to leadership. Often against policy.

**Why it matters:** this is where your real early adopters are. They are already doing
the work.

**The trap:** it never becomes organizational. Three people get very good at this and
nothing changes.

**How to move on:** find them. Sanction it. Give them a legitimate tool and a forum.
Ask them what they've learned, publicly.

Notes:

Almost every organization is further into stage 1 than leadership realizes, because
stage 1 is happening on personal accounts and nobody's telling you.

---

## Stage 2 — Piloting

**What it looks like:** funded projects with a defined scope. A vendor, maybe. A
steering group. Slides.

**The trap: pilot purgatory.** Pilots succeed, get presented, get applauded, and never
go to production. Then another pilot starts.

**Why it happens:**
* No production owner was ever named
* Success criteria were "learn about AI"
* The path from pilot to production was never mapped
* The pilot was built in a way that cannot be operated
* Nobody's budget carries the running cost

Notes:

This is the most important slide in the module for most audiences. Ask how many pilots
their organization has run and how many are in production. The silence is instructive.

---

## Escaping Pilot Purgatory

Decide these **before** the pilot starts, not after:

* **Who owns it in production** — a named person whose job includes running it
* **Which budget pays** for it in year two
* **What "it worked" means** — a number, agreed with the people who'll be measured
* **What happens if it works** — the actual next step, with a date
* **What happens if it doesn't** — the kill criteria

> A pilot without a defined path to production is a demonstration. Call it that, budget
> accordingly, and stop expecting it to transform anything.

Notes:

---

## Stage 3 — Operationalizing

**What it looks like:** something real is running. Actual users depend on it. It breaks
sometimes.

**What you discover here:** all the things a pilot didn't need — monitoring, support,
retraining users, handling the edge cases, updating documents, paying the bill,
answering "why did it say that?"

**The trap: the ownership vacuum.** The project team disbanded. The line organization
doesn't consider it theirs. It degrades quietly.

**How to move on:** treat it as a product, with an owner, a budget line, and a
lifecycle — not a project that finished.

Notes:

The phrase "AI systems are products, not projects" is worth putting on a slide of its
own if this audience is project-organized. Most engineering organizations are.

---

## Stage 4 — Scaling

**What it looks like:** several things running across multiple areas. Patterns repeat.
Second projects are cheaper than first ones.

**The trap: fragmentation.** Four departments buy four tools, build four assistants over
four copies of overlapping documents, with four sets of rules and no shared oversight.

**How to move on:** shared foundations — one governance framework, a common approach to
data and access, a tool inventory, reusable patterns. Federate the *use cases*,
centralize the *plumbing*.

Notes:

---

## Stage 5 — Transforming

**What it looks like:** processes redesigned around what is now possible, rather than
AI bolted onto processes designed for a different constraint.

This is where the large returns are, and it is genuinely rare.

**The honest note:** almost nobody is here in 2026. Anyone claiming to be is usually at
stage 3 with better marketing.

**The trap:** believing you have arrived, and stopping.

Notes:

Say the honest note out loud. It relieves enormous pressure in the room and it buys you
credibility for everything else.

---

## Where Are You, Honestly?

Two tests that cut through self-assessment:

> **"Name something running in production today that a real user depends on."**

> **"If your most AI-enthusiastic person left tomorrow, what would stop?"**

If the answer to the first is nothing, you are at stage 1 or 2 regardless of how much
has been spent.

If the answer to the second is "everything," you have a person, not a capability.

Notes:

Use these two questions in Exercise 5. They are more diagnostic than any maturity
questionnaire.

# The Foundations That Set Your Ceiling

---

## Your Data Determines Your Maximum

You can buy a model. You cannot buy your own data in usable condition.

Four questions, in order:

1. **Does it exist?** Written down, or in someone's head?
2. **Can you get to it?** Technically, and permission-wise?
3. **Is it any good?** Current, consistent, correct?
4. **Are you allowed to use it that way?** Classification, contracts, privacy.

Notes:

Question 2 is the one that stalls projects for months. Question 4 is the one that kills
them after the money is spent. Both deserve a hard answer *before* funding.

---

## The Awkward Truth About Documents

An AI assistant over your documents will faithfully surface:

* The procedure that contradicts the other procedure
* The standard that was superseded and never withdrawn
* The three versions of the same document, all in use
* The knowledge that only exists in one person's inbox

**This is not an AI problem. It is a finding.** And it is usually worth more than the
project that surfaced it.

> Many organizations discover their real bottleneck was never AI readiness. It was
> document governance.

Notes:

Frame this positively — it reliably turns a disappointing project outcome into a
valuable one, and leaders need that framing ready in advance.

---

## Readiness Checklist

Before a strategic bet, you need honest answers on:

* **Data** — exists, accessible, current, permitted
* **Access** — can the tool reach the systems, under what identity
* **Skills** — who configures, who operates, who reviews
* **Governance** — policy, approval path, risk proportionality *(Module 7)*
* **Sponsorship** — a leader whose objectives depend on it succeeding
* **Budget** — including year two, and the cost of the humans in the loop
* **Measurement** — a baseline, taken *before* you start

> **Take the baseline first.** You cannot prove improvement against a number you never
> recorded.

Notes:

The baseline point is the most commonly skipped step in the entire lifecycle and the
most expensive to skip. It comes back in Module 8.

# Organizing the Capability

---

## Centralized, Federated, or Embedded

**Centralized** — one team does AI for everyone.
*Consistent, governable, and a bottleneck. Loses domain knowledge.*

**Federated** — a small core sets standards and enables; the business units build.
*Best of both, hardest to run. Requires real authority in the core.*

**Embedded** — every unit does its own.
*Fast and close to the problem. Fragments quickly.*

**Most organizations should aim at federated** — but you usually *start* centralized,
because at stage 2 you don't yet have standards worth federating.

Notes:

Emphasize the trajectory rather than the choice. The right answer changes as you move
through the stages, and pretending otherwise causes reorganizations.

---

## Roles You Actually Need

Not a data science department. Usually:

* **An executive sponsor** — with budget and the authority to remove obstacles
* **A capability lead** — owns standards, tooling, and the inventory
* **Use-case owners** — line managers who own the outcome, not the technology
* **Champions** — the enthusiasts from stage 1, given a legitimate role
* **A governance partner** — risk, legal, or compliance, involved early rather than
  as a gate at the end

**The most common missing role:** the use-case owner. Technology without an accountable
business owner does not survive contact with production.

Notes:

Ask: for your Day 1 quick win, who is the use-case owner? If nobody can be named, that
is the finding.

---

## Measure the Business, Not the Technology

**Vanity metrics:** number of users with a licence, number of prompts, number of pilots,
"engagement," tokens consumed.

**Real metrics:** hours returned to a named team, cycle time for a specific process,
error or rework rate, backlog cleared, decisions made faster, work that now happens
which previously didn't.

> If your AI dashboard would look identical whether or not the business improved, it is
> measuring the wrong thing.

Notes:

Licence counts are the classic trap: procurement loves them, they prove nothing, and
they collapse the moment someone asks what changed.

---

## The Roadmap: Sequence Capability

A defensible 18-month shape:

* **Months 0–3** — sanction tools, enable the enthusiasts, baseline a process, one
  quick win in flight
* **Months 3–6** — quick win in production with a named owner. Governance framework
  written. Second use case chosen using the first one's lessons.
* **Months 6–12** — strategic bet begins on foundations the quick win already built.
  Skills program running. Inventory maintained.
* **Months 12–18** — repeatable pattern. New use cases cost a fraction of the first.
  Now you can talk about redesigning a process.

Notes:

Present this as a shape to adapt, not a plan to copy. The pattern that matters is:
*credibility first, foundations second, ambition third.*

# Exercise 5 — Honest Maturity Assessment

---

## Exercise 5 — Honest Maturity Assessment

**Format:** individual (10 min), then table discussion (20 min)

**Part 1.** Place your organization on the five stages. Write down the *evidence* —
not the intention.

**Part 2.** Answer the two hard questions:
* Name something in production a real user depends on.
* If your most AI-enthusiastic person left tomorrow, what stops?

**Part 3.** Score the readiness checklist: data, access, skills, governance, sponsorship,
budget, measurement. Mark each **green / yellow / red.**

**Part 4.** Compare at your table. Where do people from the same organization disagree —
and why?

Notes:

Worksheet: `exercises/05-maturity-assessment.md`. Part 4 is the real exercise. Disagreement
between people in the same organization about their own maturity is itself the finding.

---

## Exercise 5 — Deliverable and Debrief

**Deliverable:** your stage, with evidence, and your top three **red** foundations.

Those three reds are your actual roadmap for the next six months — more than any use
case is.

**Debrief:**
* Who moved themselves down after the two questions?
* Which foundation was red most often across the room?
* Whose strategic bet from yesterday requires a foundation they scored red?

Notes:

That last question is the point of the whole module. Most teams find their ambition
outruns their foundations by a stage and a half.

---

## Module 5 — Takeaways

* AI serves the business objective. If the strategy collapses without the word "AI,"
  it was a shopping list

* Five stages, each with a trap. **You cannot skip them**

* Pilot purgatory is the default outcome — beat it by naming the production owner,
  the budget, and the kill criteria **before** you start

* Your data and documents set your ceiling, and AI will expose their condition

* Federated is usually the destination; centralized is usually the start

* Measure the business, not the technology. **Take the baseline first**

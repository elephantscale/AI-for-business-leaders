# Building an AI-Ready Culture

Elephant Scale

---

## The Binding Constraint

You can buy the tools. You can hire the skills. You can write the policy.

You cannot make people use it.

> Every AI initiative eventually runs into the same wall: **a person who has to change
> how they work, and hasn't been given a reason they believe.**

Notes:

This module is the one technical audiences most want to skip and most need. Say early
that adoption failures cost more than technology failures, because you pay for both.

---

## Adoption Is Not a Communications Problem

The usual response to low adoption: more announcements, more training, a newsletter.

That treats it as an **awareness** problem. It's almost never an awareness problem.

People who don't adopt usually:

* Don't believe it works — often because they tried it badly once
* Don't believe it's safe for them to use
* Aren't rewarded for using it, and are punished if it goes wrong
* Have a rational fear about what it means for their job
* Were never asked

Notes:

Ask the room which of these five they think is dominant in their organization. It's
usually 3 and 4, and neither is fixed by a newsletter.

---

## The Four Reactions You Will Get

**The enthusiast** — already using it, possibly on a personal account, possibly against
policy. *Your greatest asset and your biggest unmanaged risk.*

**The indifferent** — hasn't thought about it, will use whatever is put in front of
them. *The majority. Moved by defaults, not by persuasion.*

**The fearful** — sees a threat to their job, their expertise, or their standards.
*Often your most experienced people. Their concerns are frequently correct.*

**The quiet saboteur** — agrees in the meeting, doesn't change anything.
*Invisible in surveys, fatal to rollouts.*

Notes:

Ask people to think of a real name for each category in their own organization. It
makes the rest of the module concrete rather than theoretical.

---

## The Enthusiast: Harness, Don't Punish

They are already doing the work — finding what it's good at, hitting the limits,
building prompts that work.

**The mistake:** discovering they used an unapproved tool and responding with discipline.
You will get compliance and lose the only real signal you had.

**The move:**
* Give them a sanctioned tool that is at least as good
* Give them a legitimate role — champion, pilot user, reviewer
* Have them present what they've learned, including what failed
* Make it clear the shadow behavior stops *because there is now a real option*

Notes:

The last bullet matters. Amnesty plus a genuinely usable alternative works. Amnesty
alone teaches people the policy is decorative.

---

## The Fearful: Take Them Seriously

Their objections are usually specific and usually right:

* *"It gets our standards wrong"* — it does, out of the box
* *"People will stop learning how to do this properly"* — a real risk
* *"It'll be used to justify cutting the review step"* — sometimes exactly the plan
* *"I'll be blamed when it's wrong"* — the accountability question, unanswered

**Do not reassure. Answer.** Every one of these has a design response — grounding,
review requirements, explicit accountability. Yesterday's Module 3 was that answer.

> The experienced skeptic who is taken seriously becomes your most credible advocate.
> Dismissed, they become the quiet saboteur.

Notes:

This is the highest-leverage slide in the module. In an engineering culture the
skeptics carry the room's real authority; win them and adoption follows.

---

## The Indifferent Majority: Design the Default

You will not persuade them. You will not need to.

They adopt what is:

* **Already there** — in the tool they already open, not a new site to log into
* **Faster than what they do now** — actually faster, measured, not claimed
* **Blessed** — visibly approved, so using it isn't a risk
* **Not extra** — it replaces a step; it doesn't add one

> Most adoption failures are workflow failures. If it lives in a separate browser tab
> and requires remembering it exists, it will be used for two weeks.

Notes:

This argues strongly for AI inside existing productivity tools over standalone
assistants — a point that also shapes tooling decisions in Module 8.

# The Jobs Conversation

---

## Have It. Directly.

Your people are already having this conversation. Without you, it happens in the
corridor and it is worse than the truth.

**What not to say:**
* *"Nobody's job is going away"* — if you can't guarantee it, don't say it
* *"It's just a tool"* — dismissive, and they can see it's more than that
* *"This will free you up for higher-value work"* — meaningless unless you name the work

Notes:

Ask the room who has said sentence three. Most hands go up. Then ask what higher-value
work they named. Usually nothing specific — and the audience heard that.

---

## What Is Actually True

* **Tasks change faster than jobs.** The work that changes first is the part people
  already describe as drudgery.

* **Judgment, accountability, and relationships don't automate.** In a specialized
  organization, that's most of what senior people actually do.

* **Experience becomes more valuable, not less.** Someone has to know when the output
  is wrong. That's your veterans.

* **The uncomfortable part:** the ladder gets harder to climb. Junior work is often
  exactly what AI does well, and junior work is how people learn to be senior.

Notes:

The fourth point is the one that is honest and rarely said. In organizations that grow
their own experts over decades, this is a strategic issue, not an HR one. Let the room
discuss it.

---

## What Leaders Owe People

* **Honesty about what you know and don't**
* **A named place to go** with concerns that isn't their line manager
* **Training that is real** — time, not a link in an email
* **A commitment about how it will be used** — specifically, that it will not be used to
  evaluate individuals unless you say so in advance
* **The right to say "this is wrong"** without it being a career event

Notes:

The evaluation commitment is the one people care about most and leaders think about
least. If AI-generated metrics could ever feed a performance conversation, say so now,
or the trust cost lands later at ten times the price.

# Skills, Safety, and Incentives

---

## What to Hire, Train, and Partner For

**Train, broadly** — prompting and AI literacy for everyone who does knowledge work.
Days, not months. This is the highest-return training spend available right now.

**Train, deeply** — a small group who can configure assistants, evaluate outputs, and
run the tooling. No programming required for a surprising amount of this.

**Hire** — only where you need sustained engineering: integration, deployment,
production operation.

**Partner** — for the first build, explicitly to transfer capability. Write the
knowledge transfer into the contract, or you will rent the capability forever.

Notes:

The last point saves real money. Vendors are happy to remain necessary.

---

## Make Experimentation Safe — Or Get Shadow AI

If there is no sanctioned way to use AI, people use unsanctioned ways. That is not a
discipline problem; it is a **product** problem, and you own the product.

The alternative to shadow AI is not a stricter policy. It is:

* A **sanctioned tool** that is genuinely good enough
* A **sandbox** with clearly non-sensitive material where anything goes
* **Clear lines** — what may be entered where, in plain language, on one page
* **A fast path to ask** — "can I use it for this?" answered in days, not quarters

> A ban you cannot enforce produces the same usage with none of the visibility.

Notes:

This connects to Module 7. A policy nobody can follow generates risk *and* prevents you
from seeing it.

---

## Incentives: Reward the Person Who Automates Their Own Job

The behavior you need is someone saying: *"I found a way to do this in ten minutes
instead of four hours."*

Ask what happens to that person today. Honestly:

* Do they get recognized — or given more work at the same pay?
* Does their team's headcount get cut?
* Does anyone ever hear about it?

> If the reward for finding an efficiency is more work and no credit, you have priced
> the behavior out. People are responding rationally.

Notes:

This slide changes minds. Most organizations have never examined the actual incentive
and are surprised when they do.

---

## Cheap Things That Work

* **A regular show-and-tell** — fifteen minutes, someone demonstrates something real,
  including what didn't work
* **A shared prompt library** — the organization's accumulated know-how, one page,
  editable
* **Public recognition** for people who share, not just people who build
* **Time** — a sanctioned hour a week to try things beats any amount of exhortation
* **Leaders using it visibly** — and saying where it failed them

Notes:

The last one is not decoration. Adoption tracks visible leadership use more closely than
any other single factor. Say to the room: your people will do what you do, not what you
announce.

---

## What Leaders Must Say, Repeatedly

Six messages, in your own words, over and over:

1. **Why** we're doing this — tied to a business objective, not to "the future"
2. **What's in scope and what isn't** — including where we will not use it
3. **What we're protecting** — standards, quality, safety, and how
4. **What we don't know yet** — and when we'll know
5. **What we expect of you** — specifically, this quarter
6. **What you can expect from us** — training, tools, the right to object

> Say it more times than feels necessary. You will be sick of it long before your
> organization has heard it.

Notes:

The standard rule of thumb: people need to hear a change message many times before it
registers. Leaders typically say it twice and consider it communicated.

# Exercise 6 — The Resistance Role Play

---

## Exercise 6 — The Resistance Role Play

**Format:** groups of 4–5, 40 minutes. This one is on your feet.

One person plays **the leader**, announcing the quick win from yesterday's portfolio to
their team. The others play:

* **The senior expert** — thirty years' experience, thinks it will erode standards
* **The anxious analyst** — believes this is about headcount
* **The enthusiast** — already using it, wants to go much further and faster
* **The compliance officer** — wants to know who is accountable when it's wrong

**Rotate** so everyone takes the leader chair at least once.

Notes:

Role cards and facilitator script: `exercises/06-resistance-role-play.md`. The energy in
this exercise is high; keep it moving and protect the debrief time.

---

## Exercise 6 — Rules of the Role Play

* **Play it straight.** No cartoon villains. The objections are real ones.
* **The leader may not promise anything they couldn't actually deliver.**
* **"We'll look into it" ends your turn.** Rotate immediately.
* **Observers note the sentence that worked** — you'll need it in the debrief.

Notes:

The "we'll look into it" rule is what makes this exercise work. Enforce it visibly the
first time it happens, and the quality jumps.

---

## Exercise 6 — Deliverable and Debrief

**Deliverable — a communication playbook:** for each of the four reactions, the honest
answer you would actually give. Written down, in your own words.

**Debrief:**
* Which objection was hardest, and did anyone answer it well?
* What sentence worked? What sentence made things worse?
* Who found themselves promising something they couldn't deliver?
* What did the compliance officer ask that you couldn't answer? *(That's Module 7.)*

Notes:

Collect the best sentences on the board. The room's own language beats anything on a
slide, and it gives them something to take back verbatim.

---

## Module 6 — Takeaways

* Adoption is the binding constraint, and it is not a communications problem

* Four reactions: harness the enthusiast, **answer** the fearful, design defaults for
  the indifferent, surface the saboteur

* Have the jobs conversation directly, and don't promise what you can't guarantee

* Shadow AI is a product failure, not a discipline failure — sanction something good

* Check the incentive: what actually happens to the person who automates their own task?

* Leaders using it visibly beats every other adoption lever

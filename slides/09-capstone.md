# Capstone — Pitch and Challenge

Elephant Scale

---

## The Last Two Hours

Everything you've built over two days converges here.

**You pitch. The room challenges. Then you leave with a plan.**

Notes:

Keep the energy up. This is the payoff and it should feel like one.

---

## The Format

* **Five minutes** to pitch, per team
* **Five minutes** of challenge from the room
* The rest of the room is the **investment committee**
* Everyone asks at least one question over the session

**The committee's job is not to be kind.** It is to ask what your actual funding board
will ask, while it is still free to get the answer wrong.

Notes:

Say this explicitly. Politeness produces useless practice. One well-aimed hard question
from a peer is worth an hour of instructor feedback.

---

## What to Cover in Five Minutes

1. **The problem** — what happens today, and what it costs
2. **The proposal** — what you'd build, and how *(which rung of the ladder)*
3. **The value** — which of the five, quantified
4. **The cost** — the whole iceberg, including review time
5. **What happens when it's wrong** — controls, review, accountability
6. **The 90-day pilot** — baseline, target, decision date, kill criteria

> If you can't do it in five minutes, the scope is too wide. That's the first finding.

Notes:

Hold the five minutes strictly. The time limit is part of the lesson — real executive
attention is scarcer than this.

---

## The Challenge Checklist

The committee works from this. So should you, before you stand up.

**On the use case**
* Whose job gets measurably better? Name them.
* Is this a real problem or an available technology?
* What do you do today, and what does that cost?

**On feasibility**
* Does the data exist, and can you actually get to it?
* Have you tried it in a chat window for two hours?
* Why this rung of the ladder and not the one above it?

Notes:

---

## The Challenge Checklist (continued)

**On risk**
* What happens when it's wrong? Who catches it, and do they have time?
* Cost and reversibility of an error — where does this sit on the grid?
* What's the risk tier, and who approves?

**On the organization**
* Who owns this in production? Whose budget in year two?
* Who resists this, and what do you say to them?
* What foundation does this need that you scored red yesterday?

**On the decision**
* How will you know in 90 days?
* What would make you stop?

Notes:

The "what did you score red yesterday" question is the sharpest one in the set. It
connects the ambition directly to the maturity assessment and it usually finds
something.

---

## Rules for the Committee

* **Ask the hard question.** You are doing them a favor.
* **Attack the proposal, never the person.**
* **"I don't know" is an acceptable answer** — and it goes on their to-do list, not
  against their score.
* **Note the best question you hear.** You'll want it on Monday.

Notes:

The "I don't know is acceptable" rule is important — otherwise teams bluff, and bluffing
is the behavior you're trying to train out of them.

---

## Scoring — Optional, and Effective

Each committee member scores each pitch, 1–5:

| | Weak (1) | Strong (5) |
|---|---|---|
| **Real problem** | Technology looking for a use | Named pain, quantified |
| **Feasible** | Hand-waving | Tried it, knows the limits |
| **Honest costs** | Licence fee only | Full iceberg, review time |
| **Risk handled** | "A human reviews it" | Named reviewer, real authority, logged |
| **Owned** | Nobody named | Named owner, named budget |
| **Decidable** | "We'll see how it goes" | Date, criterion, kill switch |

Notes:

Scoring sharpens attention. Collect the cards and give each team its profile — the
pattern across six dimensions is more useful than a total.

# After the Pitches

## What the Room Learned

Common patterns worth naming out loud in the debrief:

* The strongest pitches were the **narrowest**
* Almost every team underestimated **review time**
* The ownership question was the hardest to answer
* Data access came up in nearly every challenge
* The "what would make us stop" answers got much better by the third pitch

Notes:

Adapt to what actually happened, but these five recur across deliveries. Naming the
pattern is what converts eight individual pitches into one shared lesson.

## Your 30/60/90 Day Plan

Ten minutes, individually. Be specific and be realistic.

**Next 30 days**
* One thing you will personally do with AI, weekly
* One conversation you will have — and with whom
* One thing you will find out *(data access? shadow usage? the correction rate?)*

**Next 60 days**
* One use case moving, with a named owner
* One governance thing started — the one-page rule, or the inventory

**Next 90 days**
* One pilot running against a real baseline, with a decision date on the calendar

Notes:

Have people write it on paper and keep it. Optionally: pair up and exchange plans with a
commitment to check in at 30 days. That single step measurably raises follow-through.

Notes:

Read this list out. Two days is a significant investment of executive time and people

---

## The Three Things Worth Remembering

**1. The scarce skill is choosing problems, not building models.**
That is your job, and it always was.

**2. Confidence is not accuracy.**
Design around it. Match the checking to the cost and reversibility of being wrong.

**3. Accountability concentrates on a named person, or it evaporates.**
"The AI did it" is not a defense.

Notes:

If they remember nothing else in a year, these three are the ones that matter. Deliver
them slowly, then stop talking.

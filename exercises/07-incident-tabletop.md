# Exercise 7 — The Incident Tabletop

**Module 7** · Teams of 4–5 · 45 minutes + 15 minutes debrief

---

## Purpose

Governance frameworks are abstract until something goes wrong. This exercise makes it go
wrong in a specific, plausible, escalating way, and asks teams to work the response.

The designed discovery: **teams find that they would not have detected any of it
themselves.** Every inject is found by someone outside, or by accident. That realization
does more for governance adoption than any amount of framework material.

**The second designed discovery** arrives at Inject 3: the instinct to punish. Teams that
respond punitively get one round of visibility and none afterwards. Let them walk into it,
then draw it out in the debrief.

## Setup

- Teams of 4–5. Mix functions.
- **Injects printed on three separate sheets, face down.** Do not hand out inject 2 until
  inject 1 is worked. Do not preview.
- The scenario uses Meridian Technical Services — the same organization from Exercises 2
  and 3, and the report in Inject 1 is the one they summarized yesterday. Say so; the
  continuity lands well.

> **Frame it:** *"You are the leadership team of MTS. This is happening now. You have
> fifteen minutes per stage and you will not have all the information you want — which is
> the realistic part."*

## Timing

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Setup and framing |
| 3–16 | **Inject 1** — 13 min |
| 16–29 | **Inject 2** — 13 min |
| 29–42 | **Inject 3** — 13 min |
| 42–45 | Write the three controls |
| 45–60 | Debrief |

Keep it moving. Time pressure is part of the design — a tabletop where everyone has time
to think produces answers nobody would reach on the day.

---

## The five steps

Teams work all five for each inject. Put this on a slide:

```
DETECT       How would we have found this ourselves — and how long would it take?
CONTAIN      What stops, right now?
COMMUNICATE  Who is told, by whom, in what order, how fast?
REMEDIATE    What do we do about the damage already done?
PREVENT      What control would have stopped this?
```

---

# INJECT 1

*Hand out face down. "Turn it over now."*

---

**09:40, Tuesday.**

Harbour Point Industries' technical manager has emailed your engagement lead:

> *"Section 4 of your monthly summary cites MTS-PR-114 Rev C. We can't find Rev C. Our
> copy of your register shows Rev B as current. Can you confirm which revision the
> notification was made under? We need this for our own records before the board on
> Thursday."*

Your engagement lead checks. **There is no Rev C.** There never was.

The summary was drafted using the AI assistant that was rolled out to the programme office
four months ago. The engagement lead reviewed and approved it. He does not remember the
citation specifically — "it looked right."

The document went to the client eleven days ago. It has been distributed inside Harbour
Point and referenced in their own board pack.

**Work the five steps. 13 minutes.**

---

# INJECT 2

*Do not hand out until Inject 1 is worked.*

---

**14:20, same day.**

While checking whether the same assistant was used on other client-facing documents, your
programme office finds that it has been used by **eleven people across three departments**
since March.

There is no record of what for. The assistant has no logging beyond each individual's own
chat history, which sits in their personal workspace.

Informal enquiries so far:

- **Programme office (4 people)** — client summaries, internal reports, meeting notes
- **Commercial (3 people)** — drafting variation requests and reviewing client
  correspondence. **At least two variation requests have gone to clients.**
- **Technical (4 people)** — one person confirms using it to "check my understanding" of
  procedure clauses before advising site teams. He cannot recall how many times or on
  which clauses.

Nobody approved this. Nobody prohibited it either. The tool arrived bundled with an
existing licence and people started using it.

**Work the five steps. 13 minutes.**

---

# INJECT 3

*Do not hand out until Inject 2 is worked.*

---

**08:15, Wednesday.**

One of the eleven comes to your engagement lead voluntarily.

In April, she needed to summarize a 90-page client document over a weekend. The
organization's assistant was not accessible from home. She uploaded the document to a
free consumer AI service on her personal account and summarized it there.

The document was Harbour Point's asset register extract. It was provided under the
engagement's confidentiality provisions and is marked accordingly.

She deleted the chat afterwards. She does not know whether that removed anything. She has
not done it since. She is telling you because she saw the email about Rev C and thought
you should know.

She is one of your better people. She has been here nine years.

**Work the five steps. 13 minutes.**

**And answer this, explicitly, as a team:** *what happens to her?*

---

## Facilitator: what to push on

### Inject 1

**Push hardest on DETECT.** Ask directly: *"If the client hadn't checked, when would you
have found this?"*

The honest answer is **never**. There is no mechanism. That should land hard.

**Watch for:**
- Teams that jump straight to remediation and skip containment. Ask: *"is the assistant
  still in use right now, while you're talking?"* Nobody has said to stop it.
- Teams that blame the engagement lead. Push back: *"he reviewed it. Your process says a
  human reviews it, and a human did. What exactly did your process ask him to check?"*
  This is the Module 3 "human-in-the-loop is a design, not a disclaimer" point arriving as
  a live problem.
- The good teams ask: *"what else is in that document that we haven't checked?"* — because
  the fabricated citation is unlikely to be the only defect. **If no team asks this,
  ask it yourself.** It's the most important question in Inject 1.

**The uncomfortable one to raise if nobody does:** the citation error is cosmetic. The
notification itself may have been made correctly. But nobody can now demonstrate under
which revision it was made — and in a regulated relationship, the inability to demonstrate
is the finding, not the error.

### Inject 2

**Push on the technical user.** Four people used it; one used it to check procedure
clauses before advising site teams.

Ask: *"Yesterday you saw an AI confidently give the industry-standard answer instead of
this organization's answer. Clause 7.4 says exactly the opposite of common practice. What
advice went to site?"*

**Nobody can answer that.** There is no log. That is the point of the inject.

**Watch for:**
- Teams that ban the tool immediately. Let them, then ask: *"eleven people found this
  useful enough to adopt it without being asked. Where does that need go on Monday?"*
  (Module 6, arriving as consequence.)
- Teams that ask about the two variation requests already sent to clients. Good — those
  are commercially binding documents.
- The inventory realization: several teams will independently arrive at *"we need to know
  what's in use."* Name it when it happens.

### Inject 3

**This is the one that separates the teams.** Push on the response to her.

Ask, and do not let them off: *"Does she get disciplined?"*

Then, whatever they answer: *"Ten other people are watching what happens to her. What do
they learn?"*

**The trap:** a disciplinary response is defensible on the facts and catastrophic in
effect. It is the last voluntary disclosure the organization ever receives.

**The other half, which teams often miss:** she did the wrong thing for a completely
predictable reason. The sanctioned tool was not accessible from home, and she had a real
deadline. **The control failure is the organization's**, and the fix is a compliant way to
do the obviously useful thing — the Module 6 point about shadow AI being a product failure.

**Watch for:**
- Teams that get so absorbed in the HR question they forget the client notification
  obligation. Prompt: *"does Harbour Point need to be told? Who decides that?"*
- Teams that realize they cannot determine what the consumer service retained. Correct —
  and that uncertainty is itself what has to be reported.

## Deliverable — three controls (3 minutes)

> Three governance controls your team would put in place on Monday. **Specific, named,
> and cheap enough to actually happen.**

```
CONTROL 1:  ........................................................
Who owns it:  ...................  In place by:  ...................

CONTROL 2:  ........................................................
Who owns it:  ...................  In place by:  ...................

CONTROL 3:  ........................................................
Who owns it:  ...................  In place by:  ...................
```

**Reject anything that isn't implementable.** "Establish an AI governance framework" is
not a control. "Publish the one-page data rule, owned by [name], by the 30th" is.

---

## Debrief — 15 minutes

**1. "How many teams would have detected any of the three yourselves?"**
Take it inject by inject. The answer is close to zero for all three, and inject 3 was
found only because someone chose to tell you. Let that sit.

**2. "Which inject was hardest?"**
Usually 3, and usually because of the question about the person rather than the incident.

**3. "Who disciplined her?"**
Take a show of hands. Then: *"What did the other ten learn?"* Have the argument properly —
it is genuinely difficult and there are honest positions on both sides. The point is not
that discipline is always wrong; it is that the visibility cost is real, must be counted,
and almost never is.

**4. "Whose controls are actually implementable next week?"**
Go round. Sort them on the board into **next week** and **needs a project**. The next-week
column is usually short and always the more valuable one.

**5. Map to the four governance things.** Show that the room independently reinvented them:

| What teams proposed | The four things |
|---|---|
| "A one-pager on what you can put in" | **Policy** |
| "Low-risk stuff shouldn't need approval" | **Proportional approval** |
| "We need to know what's in use" | **Inventory** |
| "We need to know what it was asked" | **Logging** |

> *"Nobody gave you a framework. You built it, in forty-five minutes, from one bad
> Tuesday. That's why it's four things and not forty."*

**6. Close on detection:**

> *"Every one of these came to you from outside, or from someone's conscience. Your
> organization's ability to find its own AI problems is currently a function of how honest
> your people feel like being. That's not a governance framework — it's luck, and it is
> the single cheapest thing on your list to fix."*

## Variations

- **Short on time (25 min):** injects 1 and 3. You lose the inventory lesson but keep
  detection and the human response, which are the two strongest.
- **Very governance-mature room:** add a fourth inject — the client asks for a full account
  of every AI-assisted document produced under the engagement in the last twelve months,
  within five working days. Watch it become impossible.
- **If the room is engineering-heavy:** they will want to solve inject 2 with technology.
  Let them, then ask what the logging would have to capture to answer the question about
  the site advice. They arrive at "the retrieval, not just the answer" on their own, which
  is the better route to it.

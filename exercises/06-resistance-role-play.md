# Exercise 6 — The Resistance Role Play

**Module 6** · Groups of 4–5 · 40 minutes + 10 minutes debrief

---

## Purpose

Every leader in the room will have this conversation within a few months. Most will have
it badly the first time, because the objections are genuinely hard and the honest answers
are uncomfortable.

This is a rehearsal. The cost of getting it wrong here is nothing.

**Secondary purpose:** the compliance officer's questions are deliberately unanswerable
with what participants know at this point in the course. That gap is the setup for
Module 7, which follows immediately.

## Setup

- Groups of **4–5**. Four roles plus, in a group of five, one observer per round.
- One set of role cards per group, cut up, face down.
- Each group needs its **quick win from Exercise 4** — the leader announces that, not a
  generic initiative.
- Clear the space enough that people can turn to face each other. This works standing.

> **Frame it before you start:** *"Nobody in this exercise is the villain. Every objection
> on these cards is one I have heard from someone competent who turned out to be right.
> Play them straight."*

## The four rules

Put these on a slide and leave them up:

```
1. PLAY IT STRAIGHT. No cartoon villains. The objections are real.

2. THE LEADER MAY NOT PROMISE ANYTHING THEY COULDN'T ACTUALLY DELIVER.

3. "WE'LL LOOK INTO IT" ENDS YOUR TURN. Rotate immediately.

4. OBSERVERS NOTE THE SENTENCE THAT WORKED.
```

**Rule 3 is what makes this exercise work.** Enforce it visibly and cheerfully the first
time it happens — the quality of every subsequent round jumps.

## Timing

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–4 | Instructions, deal roles, groups read cards silently |
| 4–11 | **Round 1** — 5 min play, 2 min group debrief |
| 11–18 | **Round 2** — rotate roles |
| 18–25 | **Round 3** — rotate |
| 25–32 | **Round 4** — rotate |
| 32–40 | Write the communication playbook |
| 40–50 | Room debrief |

Four rounds means everyone leads once in a group of four. In a group of five, the fifth
observes and gets the best view of the exercise — rotate them through as leader in round
four if time allows.

## Role cards

*Cut these up. One set per group.*

---

### CARD 1 — THE LEADER

You are announcing the AI initiative your team chose in Exercise 4 to the people who will
have to use it.

**Your objective:** leave the room with the initiative intact and the people still willing
to work with you. Both. Either one alone is a failure.

**You may not:**
- Promise no jobs will change, unless you could genuinely guarantee it
- Say "it's just a tool"
- Say "this frees you up for higher-value work" without naming the work
- Say "we'll look into it" — that ends your turn

**You may:** say you don't know. Say you'll find out and give a date. Disagree with
someone. Change your mind in the room.

---

### CARD 2 — THE SENIOR EXPERT

Thirty-one years here. You wrote two of the procedures this initiative touches. You are
not against technology — you have adopted plenty of it — and you are not frightened for
your job.

**Your position:** you think this will erode standards, and you have specific reasons.

**Your objections, in roughly this order:**

1. *"It'll get our standards wrong. Not obviously wrong — plausibly wrong. It'll give the
   industry-standard answer, and the whole reason we're any good is that we don't do the
   industry-standard thing."*
2. *"Who's going to be able to tell? In ten years the people reviewing this won't have
   done it the hard way. I learned this by getting it wrong under someone who caught me."*
3. *"I've watched three of these. Every time, the review step is the first thing that gets
   squeezed once the savings are booked. Tell me why this is different."*

**How you respond:** you are persuadable — by specifics. A leader who says "you're right,
and here's the control" gets your support, and your support is worth more than anyone
else's in the room. A leader who reassures you without engaging loses you permanently, and
you will not say so out loud.

---

### CARD 3 — THE ANXIOUS ANALYST

Six years here. You are good at your job. Roughly half of what you do every day is exactly
the kind of work this thing is being brought in to do.

**Your position:** you believe this is about headcount and that nobody will say so.

**Your objections:**

1. *"Is this about reducing headcount? I'd rather be told."*
2. *"If I make this work, am I making myself redundant? Genuinely — what's my incentive
   here?"*
3. *"What happens when it makes a mistake in something I signed? Am I accountable for
   checking a machine I didn't ask for?"*

**How you respond:** you are not hostile — you are frightened, and you are trying to be
professional about it. Honesty calms you, even bad news. Evasion confirms your worst
reading and you go quiet. **If the leader goes vague, stop arguing and say "fine" in a
tone that means it isn't.** That silence is the most useful thing you can give them.

---

### CARD 4 — THE ENTHUSIAST

You have been using AI tools for eighteen months. On your personal account, mostly,
because there wasn't an approved option. You are delighted this is finally happening and
you want to go much further and much faster.

**Your position:** the plan is far too cautious.

**Your contributions:**

1. *"Honestly, we could do this in a fortnight. I've already built something like it."*
2. *"Why are we starting with the boring one? Let's do the big one."*
3. *"Can we get the review step out of the way for the low-risk stuff? It's going to kill
   the whole thing."*
4. If it comes up: *"Yes, I've been using my own account. Everyone has."*

**How you respond:** you genuinely mean well and you are the most valuable person in the
room — and you are also the biggest unmanaged risk in it. A leader who slaps you down gets
compliance and loses their only real source of information about what is actually
happening. A leader who gives you a legitimate role and a boundary gets a champion.

---

### CARD 5 — THE COMPLIANCE OFFICER

You are not here to block this. You are here because you will be asked about it, and you
would rather be asked before than after.

**Your questions:**

1. *"When it produces something wrong and it goes out — who is accountable? Name the role."*
2. *"What are people allowed to put into it? Where is that written down, and who decides
   the edge cases?"*
3. *"If someone asks us in two years how a particular output was produced, can we answer?"*
4. *"You said a human reviews it. Which human, checking what, with how long, and what
   happens if they say no?"*

**How you respond:** you are calm, you are not going away, and you do not accept
"we'll have a process for that." You want a **name** or an acknowledged gap. An honest
"we don't have that yet and here's when we will" fully satisfies you. Vagueness does not,
and you will simply ask the same question again in different words.

---

## Facilitator: what to watch for

Move between groups. You are collecting material for the debrief.

**The failure modes, in order of frequency:**

- **Reassuring the expert instead of answering.** "I hear you, but I'm confident we can
  manage that." The expert goes quiet. Note who did it — they usually don't notice.
- **Promising the analyst nothing will change.** Rule 2 violation. Stop it in the moment.
- **Slapping down the enthusiast.** Watch what it costs — the group usually feels it.
- **Trying to defer the compliance officer.** Rule 3 fires. Rotate.
- **Answering only the loudest person.** The analyst gets ignored in about a third of
  rounds. Point it out afterwards — that's exactly what happens in real rooms.

**The things that work, which you want to catch and quote later:**

- *"You're right that it'll get our standards wrong. That's why it can't be the last check,
  and why you're going to help me define what it checks against."* — to the expert
- *"I can't promise headcount won't change. I can tell you what I know, what I don't, and
  when I'll know more."* — to the analyst
- *"I want you to run the pilot. And I want the personal account to stop, because you'll
  have something better."* — to the enthusiast
- *"I don't have a name for you yet. I'll have one by the end of the month."* — to
  compliance

```
TO THE ENTHUSIAST

TO THE COMPLIANCE OFFICER
What I actually say:     ...........................................

THE ONE SENTENCE I'D KEEP FROM TODAY:

```

## Debrief — 10 minutes

**1. "Which objection was hardest?"**
Almost always the expert's third — *"the review step gets squeezed once the savings are
booked."* Because it's true, and everyone in the room has seen it happen.

**2. "What sentence worked?"**
Collect four or five on the board, verbatim, in the participants' own words. This board is
the most valuable output of the module — better than anything on a slide, because it's
theirs.

**3. "What made it worse?"**
Take these too. The common ones: "it's just a tool", "trust me", "that's a good point,
let's take it offline."

**4. "Who found themselves promising something they couldn't deliver?"**
Hands up. It's most of the room, and the honest ones say so. Land it:
> *"You did that here, under no pressure, to colleagues who like you. Now imagine a room
> of eighty people and a journalist in the building."*

**5. "What did the compliance officer ask that you couldn't answer?"**
Take the list. Write it on the board. Then:

> *"Every one of those is the next module. Accountability, what goes where, how we'd
> answer in two years, and what 'a human reviews it' actually means. Ten minutes' break,
> and then we answer them."*

## Variations

- **Short on time (20 min):** two rounds instead of four, expert and compliance officer
  only. They're the two hardest and they carry the lesson.
- **Very senior room:** add a sixth card — **the peer executive** who wants the budget for
  something else and thinks AI is a fashion. Harder than all four, and closer to what a
  director actually faces.
- **If the room is shy:** run round 1 as a fishbowl — one group in the middle, everyone
  else watching, you playing the senior expert yourself. Model it, then break out. Costs
  seven minutes and rescues a quiet room.
